Diabetes risk model

How well do survey answers predict diabetes, and for whom does that work less well?

A logistic regression trained on US health survey answers (BRFSS 2015). It runs in your browser. Each result shows what drove it and how far to trust it. This is a learning project, not a medical tool.

What moved the estimate most

    This is not a diagnosis or a medical opinion. For a validated Canadian screening tool, use the CANRISK questionnaire from Diabetes Canada, and talk to a health professional about any concern.

    Evidence: is it better than a simple rule, and are its percentages honest?

    All scores come from 30% of the data held back from training. The simple rule uses age and BMI only.

    AUC: how well the model ranks people with diabetes above those without (0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect). Brier: average error in the percentages (lower is better). Flagged: share of people above the 20% review line. Caught: share of people with diabetes above that line.

    Calibration: do 30% predictions come true 30% of the time?

    US vs Canada: a gap that was mostly about the inputs

    The original 2025 notebook trained models on the US survey (BRFSS) and the Canadian survey (CCHS) and found the Canadian models scored lower. Re-checking it showed the two models did not use the same inputs.

    What changed in the re-analysis

    • The US model used all 21 survey columns, including age, income and general health. The Canadian model used only blood pressure, cholesterol, BMI class, smoking, physical activity, fruit and vegetables.
    • Trained on the same kinds of inputs, the US data scores –. That is close to the Canadian result of 0.773.
    • The US label also counted prediabetes as diabetes. The Canadian label did not. The rebuilt model drops prediabetes answers.
    • So the original gap says more about inputs and labels than about the two countries.
    The Canadian survey file is not stored in this repository, so the Canadian scores above are the notebook's results and were not re-run.

    Fairness check

    Income and education are not model inputs. They are used here only to test whether the model works equally well for different groups.

    Limits and human oversight

    Use it for

    Learning and planning

    Showing how survey data relates to diabetes, and helping a public health analyst think about which groups to reach with screening.

    Do not use it for

    Decisions about a person

    Diagnosis, treatment, insurance, hiring, or any choice about an individual. Use a validated tool and a health professional.

    A person decides

    Human in control

    The model shows a percentage, the factors behind it, and its weak spots. A qualified person interprets it.

    Known limits

    • The survey asks whether a person has ever been told they have diabetes. The model describes who has diagnosed diabetes now. It does not predict who will develop it.
    • Answers are self-reported and from one year, 2015. People with undiagnosed diabetes are counted as "no".
    • Coefficients are patterns, not causes. Heavy drinking lowers the estimate, which most likely reflects who drinks and who changed habits after a diagnosis. It is not a health benefit.
    • Survey weights are not in this public file, so results describe the people who answered, not the full US population.

    If this were deployed

    • Validate on recent Canadian data before any use in Canada.
    • Recheck calibration and the fairness table each year, and after any change in the data source.
    • Set the review threshold with clinicians, weighing missed cases against extra follow-ups.
    • Keep the tool advisory, with a clear path to a health professional.

    Reliable AI checklist

    The six questions of the Reliable AI Adoption Framework, answered for this project.

    Business problem

    Understand which survey answers relate to diabetes, and whether a model built in one country can be trusted in another. Success: honest percentages and equal performance across groups.

    Data readiness

    BRFSS 2015 health indicators (CDC survey, public cleaned version). Self-reported, one year, no survey weights. Prediabetes answers removed so the label matches the Canadian survey.

    Governance and risk

    Health is a high-stakes area, so the tool is advisory and educational only. Income and education are excluded as inputs and used to audit fairness instead.

    Technology and tools

    Logistic regression, chosen over gradient boosting because it scored almost the same and every factor can be shown. No oversampling, so the percentages stay calibrated.

    People and adoption

    Each result shows the factors behind it and points people to CANRISK and a health professional. The page explains weak spots in plain words.

    Outcomes and monitoring

    Calibration and the fairness table are the checks to repeat. The next step is validation on Canadian data.
